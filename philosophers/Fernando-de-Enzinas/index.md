Fernando de Enzinas was an important representative of the style of logic taught in Paris, Alcalá, and Salamanca during the early sixteenth century. He was probably from Valladolid, since he describes himself as *Vallisoletanus* on the title pages of several works.

He pursued his early studies at the Complutense University, having been a disciple of Saint Thomas of Villanova around 1513, and a companion of Domingo de Soto.

Around the year 1518, he moved to Paris, where he completed his studies and taught Arts at the colleges of Santa Bárbara and Beauvais, as indicated by the colophon of his printed works.

In the academic year 1522–1523, he reappeared in Alcalá as regent of Summulae. He died prematurely in August 1523. The university records for 7 September 1523 state that the chair of Summulae was assigned to Master Almenara following the death of Master Enzinas.

Alvar Gómez records that Enzinas's *Summulae* were read at Alcalá, and reports from university inspections confirm their continued use. In 1534 the professor of Summulae was teaching Enzinas's *De significatione terminorum*, his assistant was reading the second part of Enzinas's *Termini*, and the regent of logic was teaching his work on syllogisms.[^2]

Enzinas also remained influential at Salamanca. Around 1530 Cristóbal de Medina used his *Summulae* in his teaching and prepared a Salamanca edition of the *Primus tractatus Summularum*. Medina described Enzinas as uniquely learned in every aspect of dialectic and praised the ingenuity and erudition of his logical writings, even though Medina had previously taught John Mair, David Dullaert, Juan de Celaya, and other authors.

## Logical Works and Thought

Enzinas's writings form what Muñoz Delgado calls an encyclopedia of the *summulist* logic of his time. They expand the elementary framework of Peter of Spain's *Summulae logicales* with the later medieval theories of consequence, supposition, opposition, relative expressions, mental language, and exponible propositions. Because Enzinas died in 1523, dates later than that refer to posthumous editions or revisions rather than newly written works.

### *Termini et principia Dialectices* (1533; Lyon, 1537)

This introductory manual survives in posthumous editions of 1533 and Lyon 1537, although Muñoz Delgado believed that an earlier edition must have existed. It is divided into three parts. The first explains the modes of knowing, propositions, consequences and their rules, definition, and division. Enzinas identifies argumentation or consequence as the subject matter of logic. The second treats signs, terms, and their principal divisions. The third considers the logical properties acquired by terms within propositions, together with syllogisms and methods of proof.

The work presents the common principles required before studying Peter of Spain and the rest of logic and philosophy. Enzinas drew heavily on Sancho Carranza de Miranda's *Progymnasmata*, especially in its final part, but criticized Carranza for treating the example as a mode of knowing. He also called for a return to Aristotle and objected to the indiscriminate mixture of logic with metaphysics and theology.

### *Primus tractatus Summularum* (1520?; Alcalá, 1523)

This work comments on Peter of Spain's first treatise, *De propositionibus*, while incorporating later logical developments. It discusses the nature of logic, names, speech, categorical and hypothetical propositions, the matter of propositions, opposition, equivalence, and conversion. Its final section is an autonomous treatment of grammatical relatives, especially the disputed question of whether a relative expression must have the same supposition as its antecedent.

An Alcalá edition appeared in 1523; Muñoz Delgado also reports an edition dated 1520 and a Salamanca edition prepared by Cristóbal de Medina between 1525 and 1530. Enzinas explains that the work condenses his much larger treatment of opposition. Medina preferred it to the manuals of Mair, Celaya, and Dullaert for teaching at Salamanca.

### *Tractatus de compositione propositionis mentalis* (1526; Lyon, 1528)

This treatise, known from editions of 1526 and Lyon 1528, addresses a central problem of nominalist logic: whether a mental proposition consists of distinct acts corresponding to its subject, predicate, and copula. Enzinas sets the Aristotelian affirmative answer against Gregory of Rimini's theory that every mental proposition is a single simple quality, and against Pierre d'Ailly's intermediate distinction between simple categorical propositions and composite hypothetical propositions.

The question leads directly to the status of syncategorematic expressions. Enzinas argues that quantifiers such as “every” and “some,” and connectives such as “and” and “or,” must have counterparts in the mind because they are significant terms. He also discusses Jerónimo Pardo's view that syncategorematic expressions signify in subordination to categorematic terms. The treatise therefore contributes to the medieval debate about mental language and the *complexe significabile*: the structure by which a proposition signifies something true or false.

### The Three Books of *Oppositiones*

Enzinas's most extensive project examined opposition between propositions in three books. The first, *Oppositionum liber primus*, treats propositions without relative pronouns or exponible signs. Its governing condition is *similitudo formae*: propositions can stand in a proper relation of opposition only when their corresponding terms have the same supposition, distribution, amplification, and quantification, and when their copulas have the same logical relation. Enzinas applies this analysis to categorical, conditional, disjunctive, copulative, and modal propositions, and concludes with conversion. He noted that imperfect manuscript copies of this book were already circulating before publication.

The second book, *Liber secundus de relativis atque oppositionibus in propositionibus in quibus ponuntur relativa* (1520), studies the supposition and truth conditions of propositions containing grammatical relatives before determining their relations of opposition. It develops at length material later condensed in the *Primus tractatus Summularum*.

The third book, *Magnorum exponibilium seu tertii libri Oppositionum*, is known from a Paris edition of 1521 and a shorter Toledo edition of 1523. It examines exclusive, exceptive, reduplicative, *incipit*, and *desinit* propositions, among others. For each kind, Enzinas considers its proper exposition, truth conditions, conversion, possible arguments, and square of opposition. He distinguishes *magna exponibilia*, whose hidden complexity arises from syncategorematic terms or verbs, from *parva exponibilia*, involving expressions such as comparatives, “infinite,” or “immediately.”

Together with Gaspar Lax and Domingo de San Juan, Enzinas made the theory of opposition an independent field of investigation rather than merely one chapter within a general logic. Precisely this abundance of distinctions caused Lax and Enzinas to be condemned as paradigms of scholastic sophistry later in the sixteenth century.

### *Opus syllogismorum* (1518)

Probably written while Enzinas taught at the Collège de Beauvais, this substantial work circulated in several editions. Its two parts study syllogisms formed respectively from ordinary propositions and from exponible propositions. The first combines Aristotelian syllogistic, the *summulist* tradition, and the medieval theory of consequence. Enzinas understands a categorical syllogism as both a formal consequence and a hypothetical proposition, requiring its component propositions to preserve the same logical form. After treating the three figures and their modes, he summarizes modal syllogistic and adds notes on Aristotle's *Prior Analytics*.

The shorter second part treats reasoning with the three principal classes of exponible proposition: exceptive, reduplicative, and exclusive. This emphasis again shows how central the logical analysis of apparently simple but implicitly complex propositions was to Enzinas's work.

## Assessment

Enzinas continued the Parisian logical teaching of the beginning of the sixteenth century and was aptly classified by Carl Prantl among the followers of John Mair. His writings preserve the teaching associated with the chair of *Prima* at Alcalá and were printed in Paris, Lyon, Alcalá, and Salamanca. His influence at Alcalá and Salamanca remained substantial until Renaissance approaches to logic became dominant.

In the *Primus tractatus Summularum*, he acknowledged that the profusion of arguments and difficulties in his two books on opposition could make them useless and superfluous for beginners, and therefore removed unnecessary material. The comment reveals both the encyclopedic ambition of his earlier works and his later concern with their practical use in teaching.
