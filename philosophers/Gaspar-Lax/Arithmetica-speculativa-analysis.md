# Analysis of Lax, _Arithmetica speculativa_ (1515)

The _Arithmetica speculativa_ is a theoretical arithmetic in twelve books. Lax wrote it at the Collège de Montaigu and dedicated it to his former student Francisco de Mello. In the dedication he says that his students had asked him to print the lectures on natural philosophy he had given them. He decided to publish arithmetical theorems and problems first, because without them "nothing firm" can be proved or refuted in the physical calculations or the mathematical inquiries (p. 7). The book is therefore meant as a foundation for calculatory physics, like Dolz's _Cunabula_ three years later.

**Aim of this page.** This is a companion to the [transcription](Arithmetica-speculativa-1515.html). It explains what each book does, what is distinctive in Lax's treatment, and how it compares with earlier and later arithmetic. It is meant to be read alongside the text and to stay considerably shorter than it.

**Planned companion.** Worked examples, exercises, and a short primer on the notation needed to do them will go on a separate page, so that this analysis stays brief. That page has not been written yet.

**References.** "p. 9" gives the image number of the Bayerische Staatsbibliothek digitization, marked `/9/` in the transcription. It is not a printed folio. "Def. 2" is a definition of Book I. Sections are numbered by book (I.1, I.2, …), so that references stay valid if the analysis is later split into one page per book. Translations from the Latin are ours.

# I. Book I: what is a number?

Book I opens with thirty definitions (pp. 8–9), followed by a commentary that so far reaches definition 10 (pp. 9–10). The commentary matters more than the definitions. The definitions are traditional. The commentary asks what they can mean if numbers are not things made of units. Section I.3 compares the result with the first axiomatic arithmetic, Peano's _Arithmetices principia_ (1889). The comparison is close because Peano also wrote in Latin, also took _unitas_ and _numerus_ as his basic words, and in 1889 also started the numbers from one rather than zero.

## I.1 The definitions

The first two definitions are Euclid's (_Elements_ VII, defs. 1–2) in their standard Latin form.

| Def. | Lax's Latin | Translation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | _Unitas est qua unaquaeque res una dicitur._ | Unity is that by which each thing is called one. |
| 2 | _Numerus est multitudo ex unitatibus composita._ | Number is a multitude composed of units. |
| 3 | _Naturalis series numerorum dicitur in qua secundum unitatis additionem fit ipsorum computatio._ | The natural series of numbers is that in which they are counted by the addition of unity. |
| 4 | _Differentia numerorum appellatur numerus quo maior abundat a minore._ | The difference of numbers is the number by which the greater exceeds the smaller. |
| 7 | _Numeri circum se positi sunt quorum differentia est unitas._ | Numbers "placed around one another" (neighbours) are those whose difference is unity. |
| 10 | _Numerus per aliquem multiplicari dicitur qui totiens sibi coacervatur quotiens in multiplicante est unitas._ | A number is multiplied by another when it is heaped up on itself as many times as there is a unit in the multiplier. |
| 13 | _Numerus aliquem numerare dicitur qui secundum aliquem multiplicatus illum producit._ | A number numbers (measures) another when, multiplied by some number, it produces it. |
| 20 | _Prima et simplex numeri pars est unitas._ | The first and simple part of number is unity. |

The rest define equal distance and neighbourhood among numbers (defs. 5–9), the product (defs. 11–12, 14), parts, aliquot parts and multiples (defs. 15–22), division and the quotient (defs. 23–27), and measure and commensurability (defs. 28–30).

On this account a number is a **collection of units**. Counting moves through the numbers by adding one unit at a time (def. 3). Multiplication is repeated heaping (def. 10), and measuring is defined from multiplication (def. 13).

## I.2 The commentary: a nominalist reading

### I.2.1 The arithmetician's units are imagined

On def. 1 Lax says that the arithmetician _imagines_ unity as distinct from the thing called one, and as indivisible, in the way the realists (_reales_) hold. Units may not exist "really and in fact" as this way of speaking assumes, but the proofs are unaffected, because _abstrahentium non est mendacium_: those who abstract do not lie (compare Aristotle, _Physics_ II.2, 193b35). Otherwise, Lax adds, "all the mathematical sciences would perish according to the way of the nominalists" (p. 9). The arithmetician may speak as a realist without being one.

### I.2.2 A number is an indivisible accident that presupposes units

On def. 2 Lax goes further. Strictly, a number is **not** made of units. It is "an indivisible accident, like unity, lacking every part", and differs from unity only because it **presupposes several units** (_praesupponens plures unitates_). The common ways of speaking must then be paraphrased "in this sense" (_ad hunc sensum_, p. 9):

- **Part.** A unit is "part" of a number because it is one of the units the number presupposes. One number is "part" of another because the second presupposes the same units and others besides.
- **Addition.** To add numbers is to "take a number presupposing as many units as all those which they presuppose" (_sumitur numerus praesupponens tot unitates quot sunt illae omnes quas illi praesupponunt_). Nothing is literally put together, since "an indivisible added to a divisible or an indivisible does not make it greater".
- **Greater and smaller.** "Properly, no number is greater or smaller than another." One is called greater because it presupposes more units.

Lax defers the full discussion to his physics, under discrete quantity.

### I.2.3 Is one a number?

Def. 2 excludes unity, since one unit is not a multitude. Boethius, however, includes unity under number as its "source and principle". Lax distinguishes two senses (pp. 9–10):

- **strict:** a multitude of units. Unity is not a number, and the first number is two;
- **broad:** "a multitude composed of units **or the principle of number**". Unity is a number, and Lax says he will often use the word this way.

He needs the broad sense. Without it, the difference of 7 and 6 would not be a number, and def. 4 would fail (p. 10).

### I.2.4 The natural series

Lax raises two questions about def. 3 (p. 10).

1. **Must the series start from one?** The question is merely verbal. If it must, def. 3 reads: "an ordering of some numbers, beginning from unity, in which each following number exceeds the one immediately preceding it by unity alone" (_quilibet numerus sequens immediate praecedentem ipsum per solam unitatem excedit_). If it need not, $10, 11, 12, \dots$ is a natural series too.
2. **Does the order matter?** The same numbers rearranged are no longer a natural series. The series is an **ordering**, not a collection.

A natural series in Lax's sense is any run of "some numbers" (_aliquorum numerorum_) rising by one. It is not the totality of all numbers.

### I.2.5 Multiplication beyond numbers

On def. 10 Lax separates three kinds of multiplication: a line by a line, which belongs to the geometer (Euclid, _Elements_ II); anything by a number; and anything by an aliquot part, deferred to Book IX. The second applies to continuous quantities as well as to numbers, so he generalizes it: multiplication of anything is "the heaping up of it on itself as many times as unity is found in the number multiplying it" (p. 10).

## I.3 Lax and Peano

### I.3.1 Peano's system

Peano takes four signs as undefined: $\mathrm{N}$, "number (positive integer)"; $1$, _unitas_; $a+1$, _sequens a_, "the successor of $a$"; and $=$. Their ideas, he says, "cannot be defined by ideas supposed known beforehand". He fixes them instead by nine axioms (§ 1). Apart from the four on equality, these are:

- **Ax. 1.** $1$ is a number.
- **Ax. 6.** If $a$ is a number, so is $a+1$.
- **Ax. 7.** $a = b$ if and only if $a+1 = b+1$.
- **Ax. 8.** $a+1 \neq 1$: one is no successor.
- **Ax. 9, induction.** A class that contains $1$, and contains $x+1$ whenever it contains $x$, contains every number.

Everything else is defined:

- **addition** by recursion: $a+(b+1) = (a+b)+1$;
- **subtraction and order:** $a < b$ when some $x$ satisfies $x+a = b$;
- **multiplication:** $a \times 1 = a$ and $a \times (b+1) = a \times b + a$;
- **divisibility:** $a$ divides $b$ when some $x$ satisfies $xa = b$.

### I.3.2 Overview

| Question | Lax (1515) | Peano (1889) |
|---|---|---|
| Basic notions | **Defined**: unity, then number as a multitude of units | **Undefined**, fixed by axioms |
| What a number is | An indivisible accident presupposing several imagined units | Not said: whatever satisfies the axioms |
| First number | Strictly two; broadly one | One (zero in later versions) |
| Successor | "Addition of unity"; each term exceeds the preceding "by unity alone" | Primitive $a+1$ |
| Different numbers, different successors; one is no successor | Not stated; follow from the picture of units | Axioms 7 and 8 |
| Induction | Absent; "and so on" (_et ita deinceps_) | Axiom 9 |
| Addition | Counting all the units presupposed | Recursion |
| Greater / smaller | Presupposing more or fewer units | Defined from addition |
| Multiplication | Heaping up as many times as there are units in the multiplier | Recursion |
| Measuring | $a$ numbers $b$ if $a$ times some number gives $b$ | $a$ divides $b$ if some $x$ satisfies $xa = b$ |

### I.3.3 Where they agree

- **Same starting point.** Lax's broad sense of number covers exactly Peano's $\mathrm{N}$ of 1889: one, two, three, …, with no zero. Lax's "exceeds the preceding by unity alone" is in words what Peano writes $a+1$ and glosses _sequens a, sive a plus 1_.
- **One must be a number.** Lax keeps needing unity as a number (I.2.3), and his broad definition is a disjunction: a multitude **or** its principle. Peano removes the disjunction by making $1$ a number by axiom. Both are pushed by the same need, that the operations stay inside the system. The same need later brought in $0$, so that $a - a$ has a value.
- **Order is essential.** Lax's rearranged series that is no longer a natural series (I.2.4) anticipates the modern view of the natural numbers as a structure, not a mere collection.
- **Inverse operations as relations.** Lax's measuring (def. 13) has the same form as Peano's divisibility: some number exists that, combined with the first, gives the second.

### I.3.4 Where they differ

**Definition versus axiom.** Lax, following Euclid, defines unity as "that by which each thing is called **one**", which presupposes the notion it defines. Peano denies that such a definition is possible and characterizes the notions by their properties. Lax goes part of the way: by treating units as imagined and appealing to _abstrahentium non est mendacium_, he detaches the proofs from what units really are. But he never lists the properties the proofs rely on.

**No induction.** Peano's ninth axiom says that there are no numbers except $1$ and what can be reached from it by repeated succession. That is what justifies proof by induction and definition by recursion. Book I has nothing like it. Its natural series is any run rising by one, not the least totality closed under adding one, and where a general pattern is needed the definitions say "and so on" (defs. 16, 21). Explicit arguments by induction come later, notably in Maurolico's _Arithmeticorum libri duo_ (1575) and in Pascal.

**Successor properties as consequences.** For Lax, different numbers have different successors, and one is the successor of nothing, because of what numbers are: collections of units, with unity "the first and simple part" (def. 20). Lax reasons from an intended model and reads the properties off it. Peano states the properties and leaves the model open.

**Addition: counting units versus recursion.** Lax's addition is **cardinal**: the sum is "a number presupposing as many units as all those which they presuppose". Read literally, this works only if the addends presuppose **different** units.

- Two apples, with units $u_1, u_2$, have the number 2. Add it to the number of two other apples, $u_3, u_4$. The units presupposed are $u_1, u_2, u_3, u_4$, and the sum is 4.
- Add the same 2 to **itself**. The units presupposed by both addends are still only $u_1, u_2$. "All those units" are two, so the sum would be 2: $2 + 2 = 2$.

In modern terms Lax's sum is the size of the **union** of the two collections of units. That equals the sum of their sizes only when the collections share no unit:

$$|A \cup B| = |A| + |B| \quad \text{only if } A \cap B = \varnothing.$$

Lax neither draws this conclusion nor discusses the case. In practice the arithmetician, who imagines his units (I.2.1), simply imagines two separate pairs. One could also read _illae omnes_ as counting each addend's units separately, even when they are the same units, which gives 4. The text does not say so. Aristotle had already asked whether units can always be added to one another (_Metaphysics_ XIII.6–8). Peano's addition never mentions units:

$$2 + 2 = 2 + (1+1) = (2+1)+1 = 3+1 = 4,$$

so nothing can coincide.

**Multiplication.** Def. 10 keeps Euclid's wording. Read literally, a number "heaped up on itself" as many times as there are units in $b$ gives $(b+1)a$, not $ba$. The intended sense, "taken as many times", is clear. Peano's $a\times(b+1) = a\times b + a$ says the same without the ambiguity. Lax, however, extends multiplication at once to continuous quantities (I.2.5).

**What numbers are.** Lax places number under the category of quantity, as an indivisible accident, and then paraphrases away most of what arithmetic seems to say about it. Peano says nothing about what numbers are. Lax's _tot … quot_ ("as many … as") contains the germ of Frege's later criterion of equinumerosity (1884), but Lax does not isolate it as the condition for two numbers being the same.

## I.4 Assessment

Book I is not an axiomatic arithmetic, and reading it as an anticipation of Peano would be anachronistic. Its definitions are Euclidean and Boethian, it has no induction, and it takes the properties of the numbers for granted. The comparison nevertheless shows four things.

1. **The same raw materials.** _Unitas_, _numerus_ and "exceeding the preceding by unity alone" are, almost word for word, the primitives Peano wrote as $1$, $\mathrm{N}$ and _sequens a_.
2. **The same pressures.** Counting one as a number so that differences have names, and treating the series as an ordering, are the pressures that shaped the modern definition of the natural numbers.
3. **The missing principle.** What separates Lax from Peano is method, not vocabulary. Lax lacks the principle of induction that turns "and so on" into a rule of proof.
4. **A semantic interest.** Lax's distinctive question is what arithmetical sentences can mean if numbers are indivisible accidents and units are only imagined. His answer, systematic paraphrase together with "those who abstract do not lie", separates doing arithmetic from deciding what numbers are. Peano's axiomatic method later made that separation explicit.
